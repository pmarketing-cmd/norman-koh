The dilemma every buyer is stuck in

Both camps argue about the entry. Nobody argues about the exit.

The new-launch camp has the showflat, the progressive payments, the pick of inventory. The resale camp has the actual unit, the actual view, the move-in date. Both sides sound right, because both sides are arguing about the purchase.

Here's what I ask buyers in every session: has anyone ever explained new sale, resale, and sub-sale to you properly, with the behavior behind each? The answer I keep hearing: “No. Nobody.” They almost buy without understanding this.

The 3-Stage Test

Every property moves through three stages. Your exit buyer is waiting at one of them.

This is the life cycle I map for every client. How long you plan to hold decides your stage, and your stage decides whether new launch or resale is right for you.

1

Stage 1: New sale (0–3 yrs)

Direct from the developer. Full inventory to pick from, progressive payments, 3–5 years to keys. For buyers who can wait, and who pick units with a deep exit pool.

2

Stage 2: TOP / MOP (3–5 yrs)

The second buyer purchases here, usually a homeowner who cannot wait for construction. This is where you typically see a natural six-figure movement upwards.

3

Stage 3: Maturity (8–12+ yrs)

Established estates. From here prices largely follow the property price index: up when the market's up, flat when it's flat. The lane for space-buyers and downsizers.

The two unspoken rules that drive the whole pattern

Nobody talks about them. Almost everybody practices them.

  1. The support price: ask 100 owners if they'll sell below what they paid. Around 90 will refuse. That refusal puts a floor under every stage. It's also why transactions dry up in a downturn before prices crash.
  2. The break-even march: stamp duty and interest add roughly $100–120K to a $1.5M purchase. Sellers don't list at cost. They list above it. That's the staircase between the stages.

Straight answers

The questions buyers ask me first.

Prices are so high now. Shouldn't I just wait?

Buyers tell me “prices are very high” year after year, and the support price keeps doing its work underneath. Waiting is sometimes right, but it's a decision with its own price tag. The honest answer depends on your stage and holding period, which is exactly what the test in the guide works out. And if cooling measures do come in, that's historically been an entry indicator, not an exit one. Developers price very sensitively after them.

Isn't there a wave of supply completing? Won't that crash prices?

Twenty thousand units completing does not mean twenty thousand sellers. Maybe 10–15% of newly TOP'd units actually reach the market. The rest are owners who simply don't sell, because 90 out of 100 owners won't sell below what they paid. Supply waves matter, but you have to count actual sellers, not units.

Isn't it better to just buy freehold?

Freehold carries a premium on top. You pay for it walking in. The government only sells 99-year land now, so freehold is scarce, but scarce isn't automatically right for your stage. And the one freehold category I consistently warn buyers off: boutique low-rise developments. They don't move the way people assume. The guide covers when each tenure actually earns its premium.

What if I buy and the market drops?

That's the first thing I protect, not the last. Two things historically happen in a downturn: the seller's stamp duty stops quick exits, and owners refuse to sell below cost, so transactions dry up before prices crash. That's the support price at work. I can't promise the market never dips. I can make sure you enter at the safe part of the cluster, with a floor under you and an exit pool that's still there when it matters.

Are you just going to push me a new launch?

No. Not every time buying new is better. I say that to clients in every session. The framework has a walk-away built into it, and I use it: if the numbers don't hold, I'll tell you, even if it costs me the deal. I'm in the largest agency, with access to every project. I have no reason to force the wrong one on you.
